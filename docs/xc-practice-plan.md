## 1. Philosophy & Guiding Principles

Cross country for this age group is not watered-down high school training. The goals are:

- **Fun first.** If kids dread coming, nothing else matters. Keep energy high, mix in games, celebrate progress loudly.
- **Build the base.** The primary adaptation we're after is aerobic development — the ability to run farther at a comfortable effort over time. Intensity is a small part of the picture at this age.
- **Accommodate the whole spectrum.** A kid who has never run a mile and a kid who ran a 5K over the summer are both on this team. Every workout must have a place for both.
- **Progress over pace.** The goal is for each athlete to be better at the end of the season than the beginning — not to win a specific time or place. Track improvement vs. the athlete's own baseline, not vs. teammates.
- **10% rule.** Never increase total weekly mileage by more than 10% from week to week. Young athletes' bones and connective tissue adapt slower than their cardiovascular system.
- **Hard/easy alternation.** No two consecutive hard days. If Tuesday is a quality workout, Thursday is easy and fun, and Saturday is a moderate long run (or another fun day).
- **Encourage multi-sport kids.** Athletes who play other sports are less likely to develop overuse injuries. Never pressure a cross country kid to quit another sport in favor of more mileage.

## 2. Skill Tiers

Every workout below includes modifications for three tiers. Coaches should assign athletes informally at the start of the season and let kids self-select up as fitness builds.

| Tier | Description | Typical starting point |
|------|-------------|----------------------|
| **Tier 1 — Builders** | New or very casual runners. May need walk/run intervals. Completing the distance is the win. | Can jog 5–10 min without stopping |
| **Tier 2 — Runners** | Can run 1–2 miles continuously at a comfortable pace. Some prior season experience. | Comfortable with a 15–20 min easy run |
| **Tier 3 — Competitors** | Can run 3+ miles. May have race experience or strong aerobic base. Looking for challenge. | Can sustain 25–30 min of continuous running |

**Rule for coaches:** Do not publicly announce tiers or label kids. Use distance or time as the differentiator within workouts ("today's goal is to run for 15 minutes — go as far as you comfortably can"). Let the workout sort itself out.

## 3. Effort Zones

Use these effort descriptions rather than pace targets. Kids at this age respond better to feel-based cues than stopwatches.

| Zone | Name | Description | Talk Test |
|------|------|-------------|-----------|
| **Z1** | Recovery / Walk-Run | Very easy. Comfortable. Could do this all day. | Full sentences, no problem |
| **Z2** | Easy / Conversational | Comfortable running pace. Slightly aware you're working. | Full sentences with small effort |
| **Z3** | Moderate / Fartlek | Comfortably hard. Aware of breathing. | Short phrases only |
| **Z4** | Hard / Tempo | Breathing hard. Sustained but controlled effort. | 2–3 words max |
| **Z5** | Sprint / Surge | All out, short burst. | No talking |

**For Tier 1 athletes:** Workouts are primarily Z1–Z2 with short Z3 bursts in games.
**For Tier 2 athletes:** Workouts hit Z2–Z3, with occasional Z4 in fartlek or tempo days.
**For Tier 3 athletes:** Full range Z2–Z4, with Z5 only in sprints and game situations.

## 4. Standard Practice Structure

Every practice follows this framework. Times are approximate for a 60-minute session.

| Segment | Duration | Purpose |
|---------|----------|---------|
| Gather & Coach Talk | 3–5 min | Roll call, preview the day, set the tone |
| Easy Warm-Up Jog | 5 min | Elevate heart rate, loosen up |
| Dynamic Warm-Up | 8–10 min | Prepare joints and muscles for intensity |
| Main Workout | 25–30 min | The day's primary training stimulus |
| Game or Fun Activity | 10–12 min | High energy, team bonding, motivation |
| Cool-Down Jog | 5 min | Flush the legs, bring heart rate down |
| Static Stretch & Debrief | 5 min | Recovery, announcements, celebrate wins |

**Total: ~60 minutes**

> **Coaches note:** The game segment is not optional. For this age group, the game IS part of the training — both in terms of motivation and in terms of getting additional aerobic work done in a way kids don't experience as running.

## 5. Dynamic Warm-Up Routine

Run this every practice before any real running effort. Takes 8–10 minutes. Do each exercise for about 20–30 meters or 30 seconds.

### Phase 1 — Jog (5 minutes)
- Easy group jog together, conversational pace (Z1–Z2)
- Keep the group together — no racing the warm-up jog

### Phase 2 — Dynamic Drills (8–10 minutes)
Perform each in sequence down a straight section (20–30m each):

1. **High Knees** — Drive knees up to hip height, quick turnover, arms pump
2. **Butt Kicks** — Heel flicks up toward glutes, stay on balls of feet
3. **Frankensteins (Leg Swings Forward)** — Walking, kick straight leg to opposite hand
4. **A-Skips** — Skip rhythm with high knee drive, land on ball of foot
5. **B-Skips** — Same as A-skip but extend the leg forward before landing
6. **Carioca (Karaoke)** — Lateral shuffle with crossover step, both directions
7. **Side Shuffles** — Low athletic position, quick lateral steps with arm swings
8. **Walking Lunges** — Full stride forward lunge, alternate legs, stay upright
9. **Lateral Leg Swings** — Hold fence/wall, swing leg side to side across body
10. **Front/Back Leg Swings** — Hold fence/wall, swing leg front to back

### Phase 3 — Strides (2 minutes)
- 2–3 x 80m strides at Z3–Z4 effort with full recovery walk back
- Focus on running tall, relaxed arms, quick feet
- This primes the neuromuscular system before the main workout

## 6. Cool-Down Routine

Never skip this. It's where a lot of the adaptation happens and where you prevent soreness the next day.

### Easy Jog (5 minutes)
- Very easy Z1 jog together as a group
- Keep it social — let kids talk and laugh

### Static Stretching (5 minutes)
Hold each stretch 20–30 seconds. Do not bounce.

1. **Standing Quad Stretch** — Pull foot to glute, balance, switch
2. **Standing Hip Flexor (Lunge Stretch)** — Deep lunge, front knee over ankle, hold
3. **Hamstring Stretch** — Seated or standing, reach for toes, flat back
4. **Calf Stretch** — Hands on wall, one leg back, heel flat on ground
5. **IT Band / Glute Stretch** — Seated figure-four or cross-leg glute stretch
6. **Chest Opener / Shoulder Stretch** — Arms across chest or clasped behind back

### Team Debrief (2–3 minutes)
- Gather in a circle
- Coach names one specific thing the team did well
- Coach names one thing to work on next time
- Optional: one athlete shares something they noticed or are proud of
- End with a team chant, clap-in, or handshake routine (let the kids create this)

## 7. The Three Practice Days

This plan is designed for three non-consecutive days per week (e.g., Tuesday, Thursday, Saturday — or Monday, Wednesday, Friday).

---

### Day 1 — Quality Day (Tuesday / Monday)
**Purpose:** The one harder workout of the week. Builds speed, pacing sense, and mental toughness.

**Options (rotate through these):**

#### Option A: Fartlek Run
Swedish for "speed play." Unstructured surges during a continuous run.
- Group runs easy together
- Coach (or athlete) randomly calls "go!" — everyone surges to Z3–Z4 for 30–60 seconds
- Coach calls "recover" — back to easy Z2
- 4–8 surges over a 15–20 min run
- *Tier 1:* Jog/walk base, walk during surges if needed
- *Tier 2:* Easy jog base, Z3 surges
- *Tier 3:* Z2 base, push to Z4 on surges

#### Option B: Interval Pyramid
Run intervals of increasing then decreasing distance with recovery jogs between.
- Structure: 1 min hard / 2 min easy / 2 min hard / 2 min easy / 3 min hard / 2 min easy / 2 min hard / 2 min easy / 1 min hard
- *Tier 1:* Walk/jog the hard portions, full walk recovery
- *Tier 2:* Run hard portions at Z3, jog recovery
- *Tier 3:* Z4 hard efforts, Z2 recovery jog

#### Option C: Hill Repeats
Find a moderate hill (30–50 seconds to run up).
- Run up at Z3–Z4, walk back down (full recovery)
- 4–8 repeats depending on tier and week
- *Tier 1:* 4 reps, walk up if needed, full recovery
- *Tier 2:* 6 reps, run up at Z3, walk/jog down
- *Tier 3:* 6–8 reps, strong Z4 effort up, jog down

#### Option D: Tempo Run (Weeks 6+ only)
A sustained moderate-hard effort. Teaches athletes to run comfortably at race pace.
- *Tier 1:* 6–8 min easy, then 3–5 min at Z3, then 5 min easy
- *Tier 2:* 5 min easy, then 8–10 min at Z3, then 5 min easy
- *Tier 3:* 5 min easy, then 12–15 min at Z3–Z4, then 5 min easy

---

### Day 2 — Easy / Fun Day (Thursday / Wednesday)
**Purpose:** Active recovery, high fun, reinforce team culture. This day should feel like a reward.

**Structure:**
- Easy Z1–Z2 run: 15–20 min total (Tier 1: 10–15 min walk/run)
- Followed by 15–20 min of a game (see Section 9)
- No intensity pressure — emphasize enjoyment

**Coaching cue for this day:** "Today we run easy and have fun. If you're breathing hard, you're going too fast."

---

### Day 3 — Long Run / Team Day (Saturday / Friday)
**Purpose:** Build aerobic base with the longest continuous effort of the week. Social, moderate pace.

**Guidelines:**
- Group runs together at the pace of the slowest runner — nobody gets left behind
- Coaches run with the group (model, don't just watch)
- Distance targets by tier and week:

| Week | Tier 1 | Tier 2 | Tier 3 |
|------|--------|--------|--------|
| 1–3 | 10–15 min easy | 15–20 min easy | 20–25 min easy |
| 4–6 | 15–20 min easy | 20–25 min easy | 25–30 min easy |
| 7–9 | 18–22 min easy | 25–30 min easy | 30–35 min easy |
| 10–12 | 15–18 min easy | 20–25 min easy | 25–30 min easy |

> Week 10–12 distance drops intentionally as the season peaks. Fresher legs for meets.

**Running together tip:** Divide into two groups max (Tiers 1–2 together, Tier 3 with a coach). Use an out-and-back route so groups return at the same time and finish as a team.

## 8. 12-Week Season Plan

### Phase 1: Base Building (Weeks 1–4)
Focus: Establish routine, build aerobic base, learn the team culture. No intensity pressure.

| Week | Day 1 (Quality) | Day 2 (Easy/Fun) | Day 3 (Long Run) | Notes |
|------|-----------------|------------------|------------------|-------|
| 1 | Fartlek (light) | Easy run + Tag game | 15–20 min group run | Baseline mile time trial on Day 1 |
| 2 | Fartlek | Easy run + Sharks & Minnows | 15–20 min group run | Emphasize warm-up habits |
| 3 | Interval Pyramid (short) | Easy run + Capture the Flag | 18–22 min group run | Check in on footwear/gear |
| 4 | Fartlek | Easy run + Band-Aid Tag | 20–25 min group run | Celebrate 4 weeks of showing up |

### Phase 2: Building Fitness (Weeks 5–8)
Focus: Add a small amount of intensity. Introduce tempo concept. Games remain a fixture.

| Week | Day 1 (Quality) | Day 2 (Easy/Fun) | Day 3 (Long Run) | Notes |
|------|-----------------|------------------|------------------|-------|
| 5 | Hill Repeats | Easy run + Relay races | 22–28 min group run | Introduce hill form cues |
| 6 | Tempo Run (intro) | Easy run + Last Person Out | 25–30 min group run | Explain "race pace" feel |
| 7 | Interval Pyramid | Easy run + Capture the Flag | 25–30 min group run | Mid-season mile time trial |
| 8 | Hill Repeats | Easy run + Ultimate Frisbee | 28–32 min group run | Celebrate mid-season PRs |

### Phase 3: Sharpening (Weeks 9–10)
Focus: Maintain fitness, sharpen race awareness, reduce fatigue going into meets.

| Week | Day 1 (Quality) | Day 2 (Easy/Fun) | Day 3 (Long Run) | Notes |
|------|-----------------|------------------|------------------|-------|
| 9 | Fartlek (longer surges) | Easy run + Cage the Lion | 28–30 min group run | Talk about race day expectations |
| 10 | Tempo Run | Easy run + Team relay | 22–25 min group run | Start reducing volume |

### Phase 4: Peak & Celebrate (Weeks 11–12)
Focus: Stay fresh, run the meets, celebrate the season.

| Week | Day 1 (Quality) | Day 2 (Easy/Fun) | Day 3 (Special) | Notes |
|------|-----------------|------------------|-----------------|-------|
| 11 | Light Fartlek | Easy run + games | **Meet Day or Scavenger Run** | Pre-meet energy is high — keep it light |
| 12 | Easy shakeout run | Rest or light fun | **Season Finale Fun Run + Party** | Final mile time trial, hand out recognitions |

## 9. Practice Games & Fun Drills

These are the core tools for Day 2 and the game portion of every practice. Rotate through them — familiarity is fine but variety keeps energy up.

Each game entry includes: what it is, what you need, how to set it up, what to say to the kids, how to run it as coach, what to watch for, and variations.

---

### Game 1: Band-Aid Tag
**Players:** Full team | **Space:** Open field with clear boundaries | **Duration:** 10–12 min | **Equipment:** None

#### What it is
A continuous tag game where getting tagged forces you to cover the tagged spot with one hand while still running. Once you're out of hands — tagged twice — you go to the "hospital" to do a penalty exercise and re-enter. Nobody is ever fully out of the game.

#### Setup
1. Mark a clear boundary on the field — cones, sidewalk edges, or a natural boundary like a fence line. Roughly 40x40 meters works for 15–20 kids.
2. Designate a "hospital" spot at one corner or edge of the field (a cone or chalk mark). This is visible and clearly outside the play area.
3. Pick 2–3 initial taggers depending on team size. For 10–15 kids, 2 taggers. For 16–25 kids, 3 taggers. Pick kids who are willing to be active and enthusiastic — this sets the energy.

#### What to say to the kids (script)
> "Here's how Band-Aid Tag works. Everyone is running at all times — no stopping, no walking. If I catch you standing still, you go to the hospital automatically. When you get tagged on any part of your body, you put one hand on that spot and keep running. If you get tagged a second time, you run to the hospital, do 10 push-ups, and then you're back in. The last person with no tags wins. Taggers — you're trying to tag as many people as possible. Ready? Go."

#### How to run it as coach
- **Stay on the outside of the play area.** You're watching for kids who stop, walk, or try to hide at the edges.
- **Call out kids who are standing still** — "Hey, you have to keep moving!" or "Hospital time if you stop!" This maintains the aerobic stimulus.
- **Watch the hospital.** Kids will try to avoid it, rush the push-ups, or claim they weren't tagged. Keep it light but enforce it — the penalty is part of the game.
- **After 3–4 minutes**, call a quick reset if the field has devolved into chaos: "Everyone freeze, new taggers are [names], go again." This resets energy.
- **End the game before it fully dies.** Stop at peak energy, not when kids are bored. 10 minutes is usually right.

#### What to watch for
- Kids hiding in corners to avoid being tagged — redirect them back to the action
- Over-competitive tagging (pushing or grabbing) — give a warning and remove from play briefly if it continues
- Slower or newer athletes getting targeted repeatedly — if you see it, quietly designate a faster athlete as "safe" for a rotation so everyone gets a breather

#### Variations
- **Double Hospital:** If you get tagged three times you do 15 jumping jacks instead of 10 push-ups.
- **Zombie mode:** Tagged athletes must keep their tagged hand up in the air while running (no covering the spot) — it looks ridiculous and kids love it.
- **Coach is a tagger:** Jump in yourself for 60 seconds. Kids love chasing a coach.

### Game 2: Cage the Lion
**Players:** Full team | **Space:** Two parallel lines 100–150m apart | **Duration:** 10–12 min | **Equipment:** Cones or markers for the two end lines

#### What it is
All runners start on one end line. One runner (the "lion") stands in the middle. On the coach's signal, everyone sprints from one end to the other — the lion tries to tag as many people as possible before they cross the far line. Tagged runners become additional lions. The last untagged runner wins.

This is essentially a large-scale sprint game with increasing difficulty each round. It generates genuine all-out effort because the stakes are clear and the field shrinks.

#### Setup
1. Mark two parallel lines using cones, chalk, or natural markers (sidewalk edges, field lines). Distance between them: 100m for younger/newer athletes, 125–150m for older/stronger groups.
2. Decide on a "safe zone" — once a runner crosses the far line, they are safe until the next round begins.
3. Pick the first lion — ideally someone enthusiastic but not the fastest kid on the team (the game gets boring fast if the lion tags everyone in the first crossing).

#### What to say to the kids (script)
> "Everyone lines up on this line. One person starts in the middle — that's the lion. When I say go, everyone has to run to the far line without getting tagged. If you get tagged, you stay in the middle and become a lion for the next round. If you make it across, you wait on the far line. We keep going back and forth until there's only one person left — that's the winner. Lions: you can't grab, only touch. Runners: you can try to fake out the lions. Ready? First lion is [name]. Go."

#### How to run it as coach
- **Stand in the middle of the field** off to one side so you can see both end lines clearly.
- **Call the start of each crossing** — "Go!" — so runners don't trickle across in ones and twos. A simultaneous start is what creates the chaos and fun.
- **Watch for disputed tags.** Have a simple rule upfront: "If a lion says they tagged you, you become a lion — no arguments." This keeps things moving.
- **Once there are 4+ lions**, the game accelerates fast. The crossings get shorter and the last few runners are sprinting hard. Let it play out — this is where the best effort happens.
- **If you have a dominant athlete** who never gets tagged and the game stalls, have them become a lion voluntarily for the final round ("All right, [name], you're so good you get to help finish this off").

#### Tier modification
Faster athletes start 2 seconds after the call — let them hear "go" but require them to count "one, two" before leaving the line. Do this openly and frame it as a compliment: "The fast kids have to give everyone else a head start — that's what makes it fair."

#### What to watch for
- Athletes cutting across field diagonally to avoid lions — establish that runners must cross the full distance
- Lions who grab or push — pull them and make them sit a round
- Kids who give up and walk — encourage them, remind them they become a lion and get to be in the action

#### Variations
- **Color lion:** Multiple lions each have a "color" (say it aloud when tagging). Tagged runners become that lion's color and can only tag on the next round during a specific sub-wave.
- **Reverse lion:** The single remaining lion at the end has to try to cross past all the other lions — can they survive?

### Game 3: Sharks and Minnows
**Players:** Full team | **Space:** Open field, roughly 40x60 meters | **Duration:** 8–10 min | **Equipment:** Cones to mark the field boundary

#### What it is
Open-space tag where one or two "sharks" chase the rest of the team ("minnows"). Tagged minnows become sharks and help chase. Runs until one minnow remains — they win and become the first shark next round.

This is the most freeform tag game in the set and gives athletes the most individual decision-making about direction and speed. It rewards agility and awareness, not just top-end speed.

#### Setup
1. Mark the field boundary clearly with cones — a rectangle works well. Emphasize that running outside the boundary equals automatic shark status.
2. Start with 1 shark for up to 15 kids, 2 sharks for 16–25 kids.
3. Give the initial shark(s) a 5-second head start warning after athletes scatter: "Minnows, spread out — ready — sharks, go!"

#### What to say to the kids (script)
> "We're playing Sharks and Minnows. Almost everyone is a minnow — you just have to survive without getting tagged. If you get tagged, you become a shark and start tagging minnows. Stay inside the cones — if you run out, you're automatically a shark. Last minnow alive wins. Sharks don't grab, just touch. Got it? [Name] and [name], you're sharks. Everyone else — spread out. Go!"

#### How to run it as coach
- **Stand at a corner or edge** where you can see the full field.
- **Enforce the boundary.** Kids will inevitably drift outside when they're panicking. Call it immediately: "[Name], you're out — you're a shark now."
- **Cheer for the final minnows.** When it gets down to 2–3 people, narrate it out loud — kids love the drama: "Two minnows left — can they survive?!" This builds energy.
- **Reset quickly.** Once a round ends, immediately name the next round's shark and restart. The momentum between rounds is where you lose kids — keep it tight.

#### What to watch for
- The game devolving into walking once there are too many sharks — call a reset if the ratio gets lopsided before the last minnow is caught
- Kids clustering in a corner instead of moving — they must keep moving ("You have to keep running — no camping in corners!")
- Sharks who get frustrated if no one is tagging them — redirect their energy ("You've got to cut off the escape routes — go to the other side!")

#### Variations
- **Shark upgrade:** When tagged, minnows become "remora fish" — they can only hop on one foot. Tagged again, they become full sharks. Adds a funny middle state.
- **Team Sharks:** Split team in half — one half is all sharks at once, other half minnows. First side to tag all minnows wins. Switch.

### Game 4: Capture the Flag — Running Edition
**Players:** Full team split into two equal sides | **Space:** Large field, 60x80m minimum | **Duration:** 15–20 min | **Equipment:** 2 flags or pinnies/cones, cones for boundaries and jail zones

#### What it is
Classic capture the flag with two additions that make it a running workout: (1) you must run at all times — no walking in your own territory, and (2) there is a jail zone where tagged athletes do a penalty exercise before a teammate can free them. This keeps everyone moving throughout the game, not just standing around.

#### Setup
1. Divide the field into two equal halves with a clear centerline (cone line or chalk).
2. Place each team's flag at the back of their territory — visible but not hidden.
3. Mark a "jail" zone in each team's territory, about 10m from the back line and off to one side.
4. Assign teams and give them 60 seconds to strategize before the game begins.
5. Key rules to establish before starting:
   - You can only be tagged in the opposing team's territory
   - When tagged, you walk to the opposing team's jail zone
   - In jail, you do 10 jumping jacks to be freed by a teammate tagging you
   - No walking anywhere in your own territory — only in the opposing team's jail
   - Flagrunner who gets tagged returns the flag to its spot; they go to jail

#### What to say to the kids (script)
> "Each team has a flag at the back of their side. Your job is to grab the other team's flag and bring it back to your side without getting tagged. If you're tagged in their territory, you walk to their jail and do 10 jumping jacks. A teammate can free you by running in and tagging you — but they can get tagged too. No walking in your own territory — everyone has to keep running at all times. The game ends when one team gets the flag all the way across the centerline. Any questions? Teams, you've got 60 seconds to plan. Go."

#### How to run it as coach
- **With 2+ coaches, assign one to each side** to watch for tagging disputes and keep the jail honest.
- **With one coach, stay near the centerline** where most of the action and disputes happen.
- **Watch for static play** — kids standing in their own territory doing nothing. Remind them they have to keep moving. This is the key coaching intervention for this game.
- **Manage jail honestly but quickly.** "10 jumping jacks, then you're free" — don't let kids slip out without completing the penalty.
- **If the game stalls** (both flags are locked down and no one is advancing), call a mid-game adjustment: "New rule for two minutes — you can't be tagged within 10 meters of the flag." This breaks the defensive deadlock.
- **If one team is dominating,** quietly redistribute one strong player at halftime of a long game.

#### What to watch for
- Flag being "hidden" in ways that make it unfindable — flags must be visible at all times
- Aggressive tagging (grabbing clothes or arms) — this is a touch-only game; pull anyone who grabs
- Kids standing in jail doing nothing instead of jumping jacks — the jail only works if the coach enforces it

#### Variations
- **Multi-flag:** Each team has 3 small flags. You win by collecting all 3, not just one.
- **Silent Capture:** No talking allowed. Forces athletes to use hand signals and visual communication — surprisingly hard and hilarious.

### Game 5: Last Person Out
**Players:** Full team | **Space:** A loop of 200–400m | **Duration:** 10–12 min | **Equipment:** Whistle

#### What it is
The entire team runs together as a pack around a loop. When the coach blows the whistle, the last person in the pack is eliminated and steps to the side. The pack continues running. The coach keeps blowing the whistle at intervals until one runner remains.

This game teaches pack running, positioning, and pacing — all directly relevant to cross country racing. Kids learn quickly that running in the middle of the pack is safer than being at the back.

#### Setup
1. Choose a loop that takes 60–90 seconds to complete — a loop around a field, a block, or a marked course. Longer loops give more time between eliminations and let the pack settle.
2. All athletes start together. They must stay on the course — no cutting across the middle to catch up.
3. Elimination is immediate — as soon as the whistle blows, whoever is dead last raises their hand, steps off the course, and cheers for the remaining runners.

#### What to say to the kids (script)
> "Everyone runs together around this loop. When I blow the whistle, whoever is last in the pack is out — raise your hand, step off, and cheer loud for the people still in. Then we go again. There's no pace requirement — the pack moves at whatever pace the group settles into. But the last person each time I blow the whistle is eliminated. The winner is the last one running. One rule: you have to stay on the course — no cutting across. Ready? Go."

#### How to run it as coach
- **Stand where you can see the full pack** or a straight section of the loop. You need to see who is last.
- **Blow the whistle at irregular intervals** — not too predictable. Sometimes wait 90 seconds, sometimes wait 3 minutes. This keeps athletes from gaming the timing.
- **Let the pack spread out naturally before you blow the first whistle.** If you blow it 20 seconds in, the elimination is essentially random. Wait until the group has sorted itself and a clear back has formed.
- **Narrate as the pack shrinks.** "Seven runners left — who's going to survive?" This keeps eliminated athletes engaged and creates drama.
- **For larger teams (20+ kids),** use two whistles per round for the first few minutes to accelerate eliminations at the start.
- **Watch for athletes who try to sprint to avoid the last position** right when they hear the whistle coming — this is the game working exactly as intended.

#### What to watch for
- Pack moving too slowly — give a minimum pace reminder ("If you can't feel your heart rate at all, pick it up a little")
- Athletes sprinting all-out to stay ahead — remind them it's about positioning, not speed
- Disputed "last place" calls — have a rule upfront: if there's a dispute, both athletes are out. This prevents arguments instantly.

#### Variations
- **Coach's whistle, athlete's choice:** Instead of last person out, the person with the widest gap behind the pack is out. This rewards running tightly together.
- **Reverse Last Person Out:** The FIRST person to lap the rest of the group gets a point. Whoever has the most points when the pack is down to 3 wins. Rewards strong runners without eliminating weaker ones early.

### Game 6: Relay Races (Varied Formats)
**Players:** Full team in groups of 3–5 | **Space:** Straight 40–60m course with a turnaround cone | **Duration:** 12–15 min | **Equipment:** Cones for lanes and turnaround, batons or pinnies for handoff (optional)

#### What it is
Classic relay racing with rotating formats that keep the game fresh. Each format adds a different challenge — some physical, some mental, some purely silly. The consistent element is that athletes are running hard and cheering for teammates.

#### Setup
1. Mark a straight course: starting line, turnaround cone at 40–60m, return to start.
2. Divide into teams of equal size (3–5 athletes each). If you have uneven numbers, one athlete runs twice in the team with fewer runners.
3. Teams line up at the start. The first runner goes out and back, tags the next runner, and so on until everyone has gone. First team to finish wins the round.
4. Announce the format before each round. Run 3–4 rounds with different formats.

#### What to say to the kids (script)
> "We're doing relay races. [X] teams of [Y] runners. First runner goes to the cone and back, tags the next person's hand — not a high five, an actual hand tag — and so on until everyone's gone. First team where everyone finishes wins. We're going to do a few different versions. Ready? Here's round one: [announce format]. Teams, line up. Go."

#### How to run it as coach
- **Stand at the start/finish line** where you can see handoffs and the order of finish.
- **Call false starts clearly** but don't penalize on the first offense — just restart that team's runner. These are kids, not an Olympic final.
- **Keep teams balanced.** Before starting, quickly assess if one team has all the fast kids. Do a quick shuffle if it's obvious — "I'm going to move [name] to [team] to make it closer."
- **Run rounds back-to-back quickly.** The energy drops if you take 2 minutes between rounds explaining the next format. Announce the format, give teams 15 seconds to set up, and go.
- **Celebrate winners but celebrate effort too.** "Team 2 wins — and Team 3, that last leg was incredible, you almost caught them."

#### Relay Formats (rotate through these)

| Format | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| **Classic** | Run to cone and back, hand tag, next runner goes |
| **Obstacle** | At the turnaround: do 5 jumping jacks, then run back |
| **Backwards** | Run out normally, run back backwards the whole way |
| **Bear crawl** | Bear crawl to the cone, sprint back |
| **Trivia** | At the turnaround, coach asks a question — answer correctly before running back. Wrong answer = do 5 push-ups then answer again |
| **Carry the egg** | Carry a water balloon or ball to the cone without dropping it. Drop = restart from where you dropped it |
| **Leapfrog** | Teams of 2 — leapfrog each other down and back |
| **Sack race** | If you have bags; otherwise, hop on one foot to the cone, two feet back |

#### What to watch for
- Athletes tagging before their teammate crosses the line — enforce "they must be at the start before you can go"
- Teams where one athlete never gets cheered for — make a point of calling out quieter kids by name during their leg
- Athletes sitting down between turns — keep energy up by making it clear the next round starts immediately

### Game 7: Fartlek Frisbee
**Players:** Full team | **Space:** Large open field, 50x70m minimum | **Duration:** 15–20 min | **Equipment:** 1 frisbee, cones for end zones

#### What it is
Ultimate Frisbee played with a cross country twist: when a team scores a point, everyone on the scoring team jogs a 60-second lap around the perimeter of the field before the next point starts. The scoring team does the recovery jog; the non-scoring team rests. This adds significant running volume while maintaining the competitive fun of the game.

The frisbee portion appeals to kids who don't think of themselves as "runners." The running is embedded in the reward structure, not presented as a chore.

#### Setup
1. Mark two end zones with cones (about 10m deep at each end).
2. Establish the boundaries — the full perimeter of the field.
3. Establish basic Ultimate Frisbee rules:
   - You can't run with the frisbee — catch it, pivot, throw
   - If the frisbee hits the ground (incomplete), possession switches
   - Score by catching the frisbee in the opponent's end zone
4. Add the XC rule: "When your team scores, everyone on your team jogs one lap around the field before the next point."

#### What to say to the kids (script)
> "We're playing Ultimate Frisbee. You can't run with the frisbee — if you catch it, plant your feet and throw. If it hits the ground, the other team gets it. Score by catching it in the end zone. Here's the cross country part: when your team scores, everyone on your team jogs one lap around the field before the next point. So scoring is good — but it also means you run. No dropping on purpose to avoid running. Got it? [Teams of equal size, assign them]. Flip to see who starts with the disc. Go."

#### How to run it as coach
- **Stand in the middle of the field** so you can see both end zones and call disputed catches/scores.
- **Enforce the no-running-with-the-frisbee rule** for the first minute, then let minor infractions go if the game is flowing well. The running, not the rules, is the goal.
- **Call the lap clearly after each score** — "Blue team scored — blue team, one lap, now. Red team, set up."
- **Watch the lap.** Kids will try to jog half the perimeter and cut across. Keep it honest — a full perimeter.
- **If a team is down big and getting discouraged,** quietly implement "scoring team gives one player to the other team for one point" to rebalance without stopping the game.
- **Keep the frisbee moving.** If a team holds it too long without throwing, invoke a 5-second count out loud: "Five, four, three, two — throw it!"

#### What to watch for
- Players who don't know how to throw a frisbee — have a 2-minute frisbee basics session before the game: grip, wrist snap, flat release. Even imperfect throws make the game work.
- Kids who cherry-pick near the end zone without playing defense — this is legal but tell teams if they don't like it, they need to guard that person
- The game stalling because no one wants to take the scoring lap — reframe it: "The team that scores the most laps today wins the real prize"

#### Variations
- **Double lap:** Scoring team does 2 laps. Increases the running dose.
- **Team fartlek:** Instead of a lap, the scoring team does a 30-second sprint at Z3–Z4 effort before the next point. More intense, shorter.

### Game 8: Hill Charge Game
**Players:** Full team in teams of 2–3 | **Space:** A moderate hill, 30–60 seconds to run up | **Duration:** 10–15 min | **Equipment:** A deck of playing cards, puzzle pieces, or any collectible objects; a mat or flat surface at the bottom

#### What it is
A competitive hill repeat game where athletes run up the hill to retrieve one item at a time and bring it back to their team's collection area at the bottom. First team to collect all their items wins.

The key insight: kids will do 6–10 genuine hill repeats without realizing it because they're focused on winning the game, not counting reps.

#### Setup
1. Choose a hill with a consistent grade — not dangerously steep, but enough to make running up it a real effort (30–60 seconds at Z3).
2. At the top of the hill, place equal piles of collectible objects — one pile per team. Playing cards (deal 10 per team), puzzle pieces, or colored blocks all work well. Objects should be spread out slightly so athletes can't grab more than one at a time.
3. At the bottom, give each team a mat, towel, or designated spot where they deposit items.
4. Rule: one item per trip, and you must run both up and down.

#### What to say to the kids (script)
> "Each team has a pile of cards at the top of the hill. Your job is to bring them down one at a time and stack them at your team spot. One card per trip — no stuffing them in your pockets. You have to run the whole way up and down. First team to get all their cards down wins. If you take more than one card, you have to put the extras back. Teams of [X], go line up. Ready? Go."

#### How to run it as coach
- **Stand at the top of the hill**, or position a second coach there if available. This is where the rules are most likely to be bent (grabbing multiple items, cheating on the pile).
- **Cheer athletes as they come up.** A coach calling out names and encouraging at the hardest point of the rep ("Come on, you're almost there!") dramatically increases effort and enjoyment.
- **Watch for kids who walk down the hill.** Running down is part of the rep and part of the fitness. Downhill running builds strength and teaches form. Remind them: "You have to run down too."
- **After one team wins,** run a second round immediately — either swap team compositions or change the object. The kids who lost will want revenge.
- **If the hill is short** (under 30 seconds), increase the number of items so teams do more reps. Target 8–10 per athlete minimum.

#### What to watch for
- Athletes grabbing two items — enforce the one-item rule strictly; cheating undermines trust fast in competitive games
- Athletes cutting the course on the way down — establish a clear descent path before the game
- An athlete who is struggling aerobically on the hill — check in quietly; it's fine to let them walk if they need to, but don't single them out publicly

#### Variations
- **Puzzle variation:** Each team is building a puzzle. Pieces are at the top, board is at the bottom. First team to complete the puzzle wins — adds a strategy element (do you grab pieces randomly or try to complete sections?)
- **Color coding:** Assign each team a color of card (hearts, diamonds, clubs, spades). They can only retrieve their color. Adds a sorting challenge at the top.
- **Relay at the top:** When you reach the top, you do 5 jumping jacks before grabbing your card. Adds a rest-period buffer that keeps athletes from overlapping on the hill.

### Game 9: Scavenger Run
**Players:** Full team in groups of 3–4 | **Space:** Park, neighborhood, or extended course | **Duration:** 20–25 min | **Equipment:** Printed or read-aloud list of items; phones for photos (optional)

#### What it is
Groups run a route through a park or neighborhood and must spot or photograph a list of specific things during the run. They must be running (not stopped or walking) when they find each item in order for it to count. Most items found wins.

This game turns a 20–25 minute easy run into an adventure. It's especially useful mid-season when athletes are getting comfortable with mileage but need a motivation reset.

#### Setup
1. Scout the route beforehand. Know roughly what's out there. Build your list based on what you know exists on the course — don't make it impossible.
2. Make groups of 3–4, mixing tiers so faster runners are with newer runners. This ensures no group gets dangerously far ahead.
3. Print one list per group or read it aloud and let them write it down. 10–15 items is right for a 20-minute run.
4. Establish the rules before you start:
   - Groups must stay together — all members must be running or jogging when an item is spotted
   - If anyone walks when you spot an item, that item doesn't count
   - All group members must see the item (no "I spotted it, trust me")
   - Photos count as proof; memory works if no phones are available
5. Set a turnaround time or turnaround distance so all groups come back at roughly the same time.

#### What to say to the kids (script)
> "You're going to run the [park/neighborhood loop/course]. Each group has a list of things to find. When your group spots one, everyone has to be running — if anyone's walking, it doesn't count. You all have to be able to see it — you can't just shout 'I saw a red door' from around a corner. If you have a phone, take a photo for proof. If not, remember where you found it. Stay in your group the whole time — nobody runs ahead. You have 22 minutes. Turnaround at [landmark/time]. Most items wins. Here's your list. Go."

#### Sample Scavenger List (customize for your area)
- A red mailbox or door
- A dog being walked
- A bird on the ground (not flying)
- Something yellow that is not a sign
- A fire hydrant
- A number 7 on any building or sign
- A person wearing a hat
- Something older than any of you (a tree, a building — must be able to explain why)
- A shadow in the shape of something recognizable
- A store or business with a name that starts with the same letter as someone in your group
- A crack in the sidewalk bigger than your hand
- A smell you can name (flower, bakery, grass, etc.) — describe it when you return

#### How to run it as coach
- **Send groups out at 30-second intervals** in slightly different directions if possible. This prevents groups from following each other and copying finds.
- **Run with the slowest or newest group** yourself, or have a parent volunteer run with them. No group should be unsupervised.
- **Call out a time check** halfway through if you have a second coach at the starting point: "15 minutes — start heading back if you haven't."
- **At the finish**, have each group share their list out loud. The honor system works fine for most items — kids will enthusiastically dispute fake claims from other groups, which is half the fun.
- **Celebrate creative finds** as much as quantity. The group that found "a shadow shaped like a rabbit" deserves recognition even if they only found 7 items.

#### What to watch for
- Groups splitting up — one fast runner surging ahead while others fall behind. Reinforce the "stay together" rule before starting. This is both a safety and a fairness issue.
- Groups cheating by walking when they spot items — this is fine to let slide if it's honest discovery; the rule is to keep everyone running, not to create stress
- Groups running the wrong direction or getting lost — know your course, brief the turnaround clearly, and have a coach stationed at any junction where wrong turns are possible

#### Variations
- **Themed lists:** All finds must be nature-related. Or all food-related. Or all things that start with the letter C.
- **Photo challenge:** Each group must take one creative group photo during their run (jumping, funny pose, in front of something relevant). Judge photos at the end for creativity.
- **Blind draw:** Each group pulls 3 "wildcard" items from a hat in addition to the standard list — these are unknown until they start running.

## 10. Team Events & Special Days

These are one-off events to punctuate the season and build team identity. Schedule roughly one per month.

---

### Time Trial Day (Week 1 and Week 7)
**What:** 1-mile time trial on a flat course. Run individually, timed by coaches.
**Purpose:** Establish baseline in Week 1. Measure improvement at mid-season. This is the primary individual progress metric for the season.
**Execution:** Run in small groups of 4–6 by approximate ability (self-selected). Coaches call out splits at the half-mile. Celebrate every PR loudly.
**Rule:** Never post results publicly or rank athletes against each other. Share results with individual athletes and their parents only.

---

### Pack Run Challenge
**What:** The entire team runs a course together. The team's "score" is based on how tightly the pack finishes — the smaller the gap between first and last, the better the score.
**Purpose:** Teaches pacing, looking out for teammates, and team-over-individual mindset.
**How:** Measure the time gap between the first and last finisher. Goal is to beat that gap the next time you do it.

---

### Team Relay Meet
**What:** Coaches set up a relay race circuit (3–4 legs of equal distance, each run by one athlete). Teams of 4 race each other.
**Purpose:** Combines competition with teamwork. High energy, everyone runs.
**Twist options:** Add a trivia question that must be answered before handing off the baton. Or add a funny obstacle (carry a water balloon, do 10 jumping jacks, etc.).

---

### "Pizza Run" or "Ice Cream Run"
**What:** A fun run from the practice location to a nearby food destination (or the destination is set up at the finish line).
**Purpose:** Pure motivation and reward. Season highlight for most kids.
**How to execute:** Pick a destination within walking/running distance, or have parents set up a finish-line spread. Run there as a team, eat together.

---

### Season Finale Celebration (Week 12)
**What:** Final mile time trial (compare to Week 1), recognition ceremony, and team party.
**What to recognize:**
- Most improved (biggest time trial improvement)
- Most consistent (best attendance)
- Best teammate (team vote)
- Most enthusiastic
- Coach's award (one per coach)
- Every athlete gets something — make it personal and specific

**Party ideas:** Parent-organized potluck, pizza, ice cream, team photos, fun relay race, let kids plan part of it.

## 11. Stats to Track

### Individual Athlete Stats
Track these per athlete across the season. Share with athletes and parents — not posted publicly.

| Stat | When to Collect | Purpose |
|------|-----------------|---------|
| **1-Mile Time Trial** | Week 1 and Week 7 (and optionally Week 12) | Primary improvement metric. Easy to run, meaningful benchmark. |
| **Resting Heart Rate** | Optional — anytime, morning before activity | Indicator of aerobic fitness improving over time. Lower = fitter. Requires athlete and parent buy-in. |
| **Perceived Effort (RPE)** | After key workouts | Ask athletes "how hard was that on a scale of 1–10?" Compare over time. Same workout at lower RPE = improved fitness. |
| **Attendance** | Every practice | Consistency is the most underrated variable in youth running. Track and celebrate it. |
| **Personal Bests** | Any timed effort or race | Track any distance a kid has run before and celebrate every PR. |

### Team Stats
| Stat | When to Collect | Purpose |
|------|-----------------|---------|
| **Pack Gap (Pack Run Challenge)** | Monthly | Measures team cohesion and pacing maturity. |
| **Attendance Rate** | Weekly | Team culture health indicator. |
| **Season Meet Results** | Race days | Standard results; compare to prior seasons. |

### What NOT to Track at This Age
- Mile-by-mile pace data obsessively (discourages kids who are slower)
- Head-to-head rankings posted publicly
- Body weight or body composition — never, ever

## 12. Safety Guidelines

### General Rules — Non-Negotiable
- **No athlete runs alone.** Always in groups of at least two, always within sight of a coach.
- **Coaches carry a phone.** Always. Have at least two parent emergency contacts saved.
- **First aid kit at every practice.** Minimum: bandages, ice pack, athletic tape, blister pads.
- **Know the course.** Walk any new course before running it with athletes. Know where it goes, where hazards are, where you can intercept a struggling runner.

### The 10% Mileage Rule
Never increase total weekly training time/distance by more than 10% in a single week. Young athletes' connective tissue (tendons, growth plates) adapts more slowly than their cardiovascular system. The heart and lungs say "ready" before the knees and ankles are.

### Hard/Easy Alternation
Never schedule two hard days back to back. The recovery day is where the adaptation actually happens. Skipping it doesn't build more fitness — it builds more injury risk.

### Weather Guidelines
| Condition | Action |
|-----------|--------|
| Heat index > 90°F | Shorten workout, run in shade, mandatory water breaks every 10 min, watch for heat exhaustion signs |
| Heat index > 95°F | Cancel outdoor run, move to shaded activity or reschedule |
| Thunderstorm / lightning | Stop immediately, move indoors or under solid shelter. No trees. Wait 30 min after last lightning. |
| Air quality index > 100 | Reduce intensity significantly. > 150: Cancel outdoor activity. |
| Wind chill < 20°F | Require proper layering or cancel. |

### Signs to Watch For — Pull an Athlete Immediately
- Limping or favoring one side mid-run
- Complaining of sharp pain (vs. general tiredness)
- Skin color change (pale, red, blue-gray)
- Dizziness, confusion, or nausea
- Athlete says they can't breathe normally
- Any chest pain or tightness

**When in doubt, pull them out.** Never push through injury at this age.

### Growth Plate Awareness
Kids ages 9–13 have open growth plates — cartilage-based areas where bones are still growing. These are vulnerable to overuse stress in ways adult bones are not. Common risk areas:
- **Osgood-Schlatter:** Pain below kneecap, at the tibial tuberosity. Common in fast-growing 10–13 year olds. Manage with reduced load, not total rest.
- **Sever's disease:** Pain at the heel (calcaneal apophysis). Very common in this age group. Requires cushioning, calf stretching, possible activity modification.

**If an athlete has recurring pain in the same spot, contact parents and recommend they see a sports medicine physician or pediatric orthopedist.** Do not coach through it.

### Hydration
- Encourage athletes to arrive hydrated (drink water throughout the day, not just before practice)
- Water break mid-practice on any day over 70°F
- Kids often don't feel thirsty until they are already dehydrated — build the habit proactively
- No sports drinks necessary for practices under 60 minutes in moderate weather

### Footwear
- Running shoes with adequate cushioning are required. Court shoes, dress shoes, and flat-soled shoes increase injury risk significantly.
- No spikes needed for cross country training. Spikes are a race-day option.
- Encourage athletes and parents to replace shoes that are visibly worn through, especially in the heel.

### Communication Protocol
- Before each practice, confirm headcount against your roster
- If a parent is late picking up, two coaches wait with the athlete — never leave a child alone
- Any injury that involves a fall, head contact, or joint pain beyond soreness must be reported to parents at pickup and documented
